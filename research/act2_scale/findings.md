## Methodological Framework

**This project is DESCRIPTIVE AND EXPLORATORY ONLY.** The data presented below documents observable patterns in immigrant entrepreneurship and innovation. We make no causal claims (e.g., "immigration causes innovation") and do not employ predictive models. Where existing studies (NFAP, Brookings, NBER) report projections or estimates, we cite them as reference points from those authors, not as our own original analysis.

**Key limitations acknowledged throughout:**
- "Founded by immigrants" definitions vary across sources (AIC includes children of immigrants; NFAP focuses on first-generation founders)
- Survivorship bias: We observe successful companies that exist today; we cannot observe failed immigrant-founded ventures at the same rate
- Fortune 500 inclusion is based on revenue ranking, which shifts annually
- Unicorn valuations are private-market estimates, not audited figures
- Patent data cannot directly identify inventor nationality from USPTO filings (see Section 5 caveats)
- Visa pathway data is not systematically available (see Section 2 caveats)

## 1. Fortune 500 Companies Founded by Immigrants or Their Children

### Headline Finding

In 2025, **46.2% of Fortune 500 companies (231 out of 500)** were founded by immigrants or their children -- the highest level recorded since the American Immigration Council began tracking this metric in 2011.

### Breakdown

- **109 companies** were founded by first-generation immigrants
- **122 companies** were founded by children of immigrants
- In 2024, the figure was 45.6% (228 companies)
- Among the 14 companies that appeared on the Fortune 500 list for the first time in 2025, 10 were founded by immigrants or their children (including DoorDash and EchoStar)

### Industry Concentration

Immigrants and their children founded disproportionate shares of Fortune 500 companies in the following sectors:

| Sector | Immigrant-Founded Share | Companies (of Total) |
|--------|------------------------|---------------------|
| Professional & Other Services | 80.0% | 8 of 10 |
| Manufacturing | 65.6% | 40 of 61 |
| Information | 57.5% | 23 of 40 |
| Entertainment, Accommodations & Food Services | 55.0% | 11 of 20 |
| Retail | 52.8% | 19 of 36 |

**Note:** The AIC report provides detailed breakdowns for these top five sectors. Additional sectors (finance, healthcare, transportation, energy, mining, wholesale, construction) are present in the full dataset but sector-level percentages for those categories were not separately reported in the publicly available summary. We present only what the source reports.

### Economic Impact (FY 2024)

- **$8.6 trillion in combined revenue** -- if these 231 companies were a country, their combined revenue would rank as the **3rd-largest economy globally**, behind only the United States ($28.8T GDP) and China ($18.5T GDP)
- **15.4 million employees worldwide** -- comparable to the population of the 5th-largest U.S. state

### Geographic Concentration

- New York leads with approximately 34 immigrant-founded/descended Fortune 500 headquarters
- California follows with approximately 30
- Followed by Texas, Illinois, Florida, and Virginia

### Methodological Caveats

1. **Definition scope:** The AIC counts both first-generation immigrants AND children of immigrants. When the report says "46.2%," roughly half of that figure (122 of 231) refers to second-generation Americans. Other sources (NFAP, Stanford) often count only first-generation immigrants, producing lower percentages. These are not contradictory -- they use different definitions.
2. **Historical founder attribution:** Some companies on the list were founded over a century ago (AT&T by Alexander Graham Bell, 1885; Colgate by William Colgate, 1806). The immigration context of the 19th century differs fundamentally from today's regulatory environment. The AIC report does not distinguish between eras.
3. **Revenue attribution:** The $8.6 trillion figure represents total company revenue, not revenue attributable to the immigrant founder's contribution. Many of these companies have changed ownership, leadership, and business models many times since founding.
4. **Survivorship bias:** We observe only companies that survived to reach Fortune 500 status. The failure rate of immigrant-founded companies is not captured in this metric.

### Visa Pathways

The NFAP 2022 report identified **174 international students** who became founders or co-founders of U.S. billion-dollar companies. These founders typically entered the U.S. on student visas (F-1) and transitioned to H-1B work visas and eventually employer-sponsored green cards.

**CRITICAL CAVEAT ON VISA DATA:** Visa pathway information is NOT systematically available. USCIS does not publish individual visa records, and the NFAP findings are based on publicly available biographical information and self-reported data. This means:
- The sample has significant **survivorship bias** -- we only know the visa histories of founders who succeeded and became public figures
- Founders who came on tourist visas, diversity visas, or through family sponsorship and later failed are invisible in this data
- The NFAP's visa pathway findings should be understood as a small-sample descriptive portrait of successful founders, not a representative sample of all immigrant entrepreneurs
- There is no dedicated "startup visa" in U.S. immigration law; the pathways identified (student visa to H-1B to green card) represent the routes that happened to work for these specific individuals

### Methodological Caveats

1. **Valuation uncertainty:** Unicorn valuations are private-market estimates based on the most recent funding round. They are not equivalent to public market capitalizations and can be significantly inflated or deflated relative to actual fair market value.
2. **Temporal snapshot:** The NFAP 2022 data captures a moment during the 2021-2022 venture capital boom. Many unicorn valuations have since shifted significantly. We present the 2022 data as reported.
3. **Definition of "founder":** NFAP counts companies with "at least one immigrant founder." In companies with multiple co-founders, it is not always clear what proportion of the founding contribution came from the immigrant co-founder(s).
4. **Country of origin vs. nationality:** Some founders have dual citizenship or lived in multiple countries before immigrating. The "country of origin" listed reflects the country from which they immigrated to the U.S., per NFAP methodology.

## 3. Entrepreneurship Rates: Immigrant vs. Native-Born (Kauffman Foundation)

### New Entrepreneur Rate Comparison

The Kauffman Indicators of Entrepreneurship consistently show immigrants start businesses at significantly higher rates than native-born Americans.

| Year | Immigrant Rate | Native-Born Rate | Immigrant Multiple |
|------|---------------|-----------------|-------------------:|
| 2020 | 0.59% | 0.34% | 1.7x |
| 2021 | 0.58% | 0.32% | 1.8x |
| 2023 | 0.67% | 0.28% | 2.4x |

**Interpretation:** In 2023, for every 1,000 immigrants in the U.S. labor force, approximately 6.7 started a new business in a given month, compared to 2.8 per 1,000 native-born Americans.

### Share of New Entrepreneurs Who Are Immigrants

| Year | Immigrant Share of New Entrepreneurs |
|------|-------------------------------------:|
| 1996 | 13.3% |
| 2014 | 28.5% |
| 2019 | 25.4% |
| 2020-2023 | ~22-29% (varies by year) |

### MIT / American Economic Review Study (Azoulay et al., 2022)

A rigorous study using U.S. Census Bureau administrative data (Longitudinal Employer-Household Dynamics, covering 1.02 million businesses founded 2005-2010) found:
- Immigrants are **80% more likely** to found a firm than U.S.-born citizens
- Immigrant-founded firms create **42% more jobs** relative to each population group's size
- Average immigrant-founded firm has approximately 1% more employees than native-born founded firms

### Overall Immigrant Entrepreneurship Share

| Metric | Value | Year |
|--------|-------|------|
| Immigrant share of all U.S. business owners | 21.7% | 2019 |
| Immigrants as share of U.S. population | ~14-15% | 2020-2024 |
| Immigrants as share of U.S. labor force | 17.1% | 2019 |

### Key Insight

Despite comprising approximately 14-15% of the U.S. population, immigrants account for approximately 22-29% of new entrepreneurs -- roughly **twice their population share**. The gap has been widening over time.

### Methodological Caveats

1. **Rate definition:** The Kauffman "new entrepreneur rate" measures the percentage of the population not previously self-employed who started a business in a given month. This captures business formation intent, not long-term business survival.
2. **Selection effects:** Higher immigrant entrepreneurship rates may partly reflect selection effects -- the immigration process itself may select for individuals with higher risk tolerance, ambition, and resourcefulness. This is a description of a pattern, not a causal claim about immigration policy.
3. **Necessity vs. opportunity entrepreneurship:** Not all immigrant entrepreneurship reflects high-growth innovation. Some immigrant entrepreneurship is "necessity-driven" (limited formal employment options due to visa restrictions, credential non-recognition, or language barriers). The Kauffman data includes both types. The "opportunity share" metric helps distinguish these, but it is an imperfect measure.
4. **Year-to-year variation:** The jump from 1.7x in 2020 to 2.4x in 2023 partly reflects the denominator (native-born rate declining from 0.34% to 0.28%) as much as the numerator (immigrant rate rising). Both trends matter.
5. **The Azoulay et al. study** is based on firm formations from 2005-2010 and was published in 2022. The 80% figure is a widely cited headline number, but the study itself notes significant variation by industry and firm type.

## 4. Patent and Innovation Data

### NBER Working Paper 30797: Key Findings (Bernstein, Diamond, Jiranaphawiboon, McQuade, Pousada)

**IMPORTANT:** The findings below are reported by the authors of NBER Working Paper 30797. We cite their published analysis. We do NOT claim to have independently verified these figures or performed original patent-nationality linkage. Inventor nationality is not directly recorded in USPTO filings; the NBER researchers used age-of-SSN-assignment as a proxy for immigrant status, which is an innovative but imperfect identification strategy.

**Study period:** 1990-2016, analyzing U.S. Patent and Trademark Office records.

| Metric | Value | Detail |
|--------|------:|--------|
| Immigrant share of all U.S. inventors | 16% | While immigrants comprise ~14% of population |
| Immigrant share of total patents | 23% | Direct patent authorship |
| Citation-weighted patent share | 24% | Patents weighted by forward citations received |
| Market-value-weighted patent share | 25% | Patents weighted by stock market reaction to grant |
| Patents co-authored with native-born inventors | 13% | Immigrant-native collaboration |
| **Total innovation contribution (direct + indirect)** | **36%** | Direct authorship (23%) + collaboration with native-born (13%) |

### Innovation Spillover Effects (as reported by NBER)

- The authors find that immigrant inventors create stronger innovation productivity spillovers on their collaborators compared to U.S.-born inventors
- Using variation from premature inventor deaths as a natural experiment, they estimate that immigrants' indirect spillover effects account for more than twice their direct productivity contribution
- The authors estimate immigrants are responsible for approximately 32% of aggregate innovation, over half of which is attributed to human capital externalities on U.S.-born collaborators

### Historical Trend (as reported by NBER)

- In 1975: more than 91% of U.S.-based patents were associated with Anglo-Saxon or European ethnic names
- By 2015: these groups accounted for 72% of patents
- This shift reflects increasing diversification of the inventor base

### Methodological Caveats (CRITICAL)

1. **Inventor nationality is NOT directly recorded in USPTO filings.** The NBER researchers used a creative identification strategy: the age at which an inventor's Social Security Number (SSN) was assigned. SSNs assigned after childhood are used as a proxy for immigrant status. This is an ingenious approach but is not the same as having verified immigration records.
2. **The 36% figure combines direct and indirect contributions.** The indirect contribution (collaboration with native-born inventors) is estimated using econometric models with assumptions about knowledge spillovers. The 23% direct figure is more straightforward.
3. **Study period ends in 2016.** The innovation landscape has changed significantly since then, particularly with the growth of AI research and changes in immigration policy.
4. **We cannot independently verify these findings.** Patent-nationality linkage requires access to restricted SSA/USPTO merged data. We present the NBER findings as a reference point from a peer-reviewed working paper published through a major economics research bureau, authored by researchers at Stanford, Wharton, and HBS.
5. **The ITIF summary** (which reports "immigrant inventors produce 23% of US patents") cites the same NBER paper. It is not an independent confirmation.

## 7. Cross-Cutting Patterns

### The "Immigrant Premium" in Innovation (Descriptive Summary)

Multiple independent data sources converge on a consistent descriptive pattern: immigrants contribute to U.S. innovation and entrepreneurship at rates that exceed their population share:

| Metric | Value | Population Baseline (~14-15%) | Ratio |
|--------|------:|------------------------------:|------:|
| Share of U.S. labor force | 17.1% | 14-15% | ~1.2x |
| Share of all U.S. business owners | 21.7% | 14-15% | ~1.5x |
| Share of new entrepreneurs | ~25% | 14-15% | ~1.8x |
| Share of U.S. patents (direct) | 23% | 14-15% | ~1.6x |
| Share of U.S. innovation (direct + spillover) | 36% | 14-15% | ~2.5x |
| Share of Fortune 500 founders (incl. 2nd gen) | 46.2% | 14-15% | ~3.2x |
| Share of unicorn startup founders | 55% | 14-15% | ~3.8x |

**IMPORTANT CAVEAT:** This table combines metrics from different sources, time periods, and methodologies. The metrics are not directly comparable:
- Fortune 500 and unicorn data include historical founders (some from the 1800s), while patent and entrepreneurship rates reflect recent activity
- The Fortune 500 figure includes second-generation Americans; patent and entrepreneurship data focus on first-generation immigrants
- Each metric has its own definition of "immigrant" and its own limitations as described in the relevant sections above
- The ascending pattern in the "Ratio" column is real but should not be overinterpreted -- each metric measures something different

### Technology Sector Dominance

Approximately one-quarter of engineering and technology companies started in the U.S. between 2006 and 2012 had at least one key founder who was an immigrant, generating approximately $63 billion in sales and employing approximately 560,000 workers in 2012 (Kauffman Foundation).

## 9. What This Data Does and Does Not Show

### What it shows (descriptive):
- Immigrants and their children are associated with a disproportionate share of large, successful U.S. companies
- Immigrant entrepreneurship rates are consistently higher than native-born rates across multiple measurement approaches
- Published research (NBER) finds immigrants authored a disproportionate share of U.S. patents during 1990-2016
- The pattern holds across multiple independent datasets and time periods

### What it does NOT show (and we do NOT claim):
- That immigration *causes* more innovation (correlation is not causation; selection effects are significant)
- That increasing immigration would proportionally increase innovation (policy extrapolation requires causal models we do not build)
- That all immigrant entrepreneurs are high-growth innovators (many are necessity-driven small businesses)
- That we can independently verify patent-nationality linkages (we rely on NBER's published methodology)
- That visa pathway data is representative (it suffers from severe survivorship bias)
- That market capitalization can be "attributed" to a founder's immigrant status
