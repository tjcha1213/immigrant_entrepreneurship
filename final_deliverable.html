Executive Summary

This report presents a comprehensive analysis of how immigrant entrepreneurs have shaped the United States economy, innovation landscape, and key industry sectors. Drawing on data visualizations and primary research compiled under the Harvard Data Visualization 2026 Class project, the findings demonstrate a consistent and irrefutable pattern: immigrants are disproportionately responsible for the most consequential economic value creation in modern American history.

Immigrants constitute approximately 14% of the U.S. population. Yet across every measure of innovation and entrepreneurship — from Fortune 500 founding to patent authorship, from unicorn startups to transformative technology — their contribution dwarfs their share of the population. The charts and data in this report document that structural outperformance across five dimensions: scale, ecosystem geography, visa and policy pathways, economic consequences of restriction, and comparative global competition for talent.

These four numbers establish a foundational paradox that anchors the entire research: immigrants are 14% of the population but are responsible for nearly half of the U.S.'s most consequential business creation and more than a third of its technological invention. The gap between demographic share and economic contribution is not marginal — it is threefold to fourfold.

Fortune 500: Who Built Them?

The immigrant-founded segment of the Fortune 500 is not a small slice — it is nearly half the pie. When measured by revenue rather than headcount, the proportion is even more pronounced, because immigrant-founded companies disproportionately dominate the most capital-intensive, high-growth sectors: technology, pharmaceuticals, retail, and financial services.

The Immigrant Premium

The "premium" framing is crucial. It reframes the immigration debate from a cost-benefit welfare question to an investment-return question. The data does not merely show that immigrants contribute — it shows that each immigrant entrepreneur generates economic output at multiples of the per-capita American average. In pure economic terms, the data implies that the optimal policy would maximize, not restrict, this input.

The geographic concentration of immigrant founder impact in a handful of U.S. metro areas has important policy implications. These hubs function as innovation ecosystems where immigrant talent, domestic capital, research universities, and established technology companies interact in dense, productive networks. Policies that disrupt the inflow of talent — H-1B restrictions, visa lottery backlogs, application fee increases — disproportionately affect these specific geographies and the broader national innovation capacity they represent.

How They Got Here: Visa Pathways

72% of immigrant AI startup founders first arrived on student visas (F-1). Source: CSET Georgetown

This single data point has enormous policy implications. The F-1 to founder pipeline is the primary conduit through which the United States converts global educational talent into domestic economic value. The typical pathway:

  1. Talented student arrives on F-1 from India, China, Israel, or elsewhere
  2. Completes undergraduate or graduate STEM degree at a U.S. research university
  3. Pursues Optional Practical Training (OPT) or CPT at a U.S. technology firm
  4. Transitions to H-1B visa for extended work authorization
  5. Founds or co-founds a startup — often leveraging networks formed at university or in early-career roles
  6. Attempts to obtain employment-based green card, often waiting 10–13 years if born in India

The vulnerability of this pipeline is significant. Any policy that restricts F-1 visa issuance, reduces OPT duration, tightens H-1B criteria, or increases the cost of employment-based green card applications directly attacks the foundational mechanism by which America's most consequential companies are created.

Act IV

The Policy Reality

The fourth section is the most analytically urgent. It documents the policy environment that increasingly threatens the immigrant founder pipeline — tracking H-1B denial rate volatility, green card backlogs, and early indicators of a global talent exodus from the United States.

The Denial Rate Rollercoaster

The volatility itself is economically damaging. Multinational companies cannot build stable hiring pipelines around a visa system whose denial rate swings from 5% to 30% and back depending on the political calendar. The H-1B system functions less as a stable immigration pathway than as a political instrument, weaponized differently by each administration.

The proposed $100,000 application fee would represent a qualitative change in the character of H-1B policy. At that price point, the visa would become accessible only to large corporations, effectively eliminating the small business, startup, and academic research segments of H-1B users that are disproportionately responsible for frontier innovation.

The Green Card Trap

The green card trap operates as a structural tax on the most in-demand immigrant talent. An Indian-born software engineer or biotech researcher who arrives on an F-1 visa, transitions to H-1B, and begins the green card process in their late 20s may reach their mid-40s — the peak entrepreneurial years — still in limbo, legally constrained in their ability to change employers, start companies, or pursue independent ventures.

The research identifies the backlog as a structural deterrent to the very immigrant entrepreneurship it is trying to encourage. Many of the immigrant founders who built America's greatest companies did so during the relatively brief window between arriving on student visas and becoming trapped in the green card queue — a window that is narrowing as backlogs grow.

Act V

So What?

The final section answers the "so what" question with economic modeling, historical precedent, comparative international data, and a systematic debunking of the most common anti-immigration myths.

GDP Impact: Three Scenarios (2025–2035)

The asymmetry is instructive: the downside of restriction (-$12.1 trillion) is 26 times larger than the upside modeled from targeted reform (+$456 billion from H-1B expansion alone). This reflects the nonlinear, ecosystem-dependent nature of innovation: removing key inputs from a complex innovation ecosystem does not merely reduce output proportionally — it collapses entire supply chains of talent, collaboration, and commercial spillover.

History's Warning: The 1920s Restriction

The most alarming implication of the 1920s data is the 40-year recovery period. The United States did not simply pause innovation — it fell behind at a critical juncture in the development of nuclear technology, electronics, and computing. Recovery required the post-WWII brain drain from Europe (itself a product of Nazi persecution), the GI Bill's expansion of domestic STEM education, and the Cold War imperative to invest in science. None of these emergency levers are available today.

Conclusions & Policy Implications

The research assembled in Built by Immigrants, Threatened by Policy constitutes one of the most comprehensive visual and quantitative cases ever made for the economic necessity of high-skilled immigration to the United States. Its conclusions, grounded in peer-reviewed research and primary data from NFAP, NBER, Brookings, Hoover, Cato, Georgetown CSET, and other leading institutions, are unambiguous.

What the Data Shows

  • Immigrant entrepreneurs have built 46.2% of Fortune 500 companies, 55% of unicorn startups, and contributed to 36% of all U.S. patents, despite being 14% of the population.
  • The top six immigrant-connected companies alone account for $13.8 trillion in market capitalization — approximately 23% of the entire S&P 500.
  • These companies directly employ 15.4 million Americans and generate $8.6 trillion in annual revenue.
  • The primary pipeline — F-1 student visa to H-1B to green card — is increasingly dysfunctional, with Indian nationals facing 10–13 year green card waits and H-1B denial rates swinging wildly based on political cycles.
  • Evidence of talent flight is mounting: 82% of employers have lost foreign workers to visa problems, 75%+ of U.S. scientists are considering leaving, and Canada's Startup Visa approvals have grown 407%.
  • Historical precedent (1924 Immigration Act) demonstrates that restriction produces a 30% decline in U.S. invention — with effects lasting 40 years.
  • The economic cost of continued restriction is modeled at -$12.1 trillion in GDP and -15.7 million jobs over 2025–2035.

Policy Recommendations Implied by the Data

  1. Create a dedicated U.S. Startup Visa with clear eligibility criteria, a fast-track pathway, and legislative permanence.
  2. Eliminate or substantially reform the per-country green card cap — the single most irrational structural barrier in the current system.
  3. Stabilize the H-1B program with a predictable, merit-based adjudication system insulated from political cycle volatility.
  4. Reject the proposed $100,000 H-1B application fee — a measure that would effectively restrict the visa to large corporations and eliminate its role in startup ecosystems.
  5. Expand OPT/STEM OPT access and duration to preserve the F-1 to founder pipeline.
  6. Model immigration policy on the competitiveness frameworks of Canada, the UK, Singapore, and Germany — jurisdictions actively building the infrastructure to absorb talent America turns away.
